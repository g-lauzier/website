I created [GO Raiding](https://goraiding.com) to examine how Pokémon GO players experienced the April 2023 changes to remote raiding. The project connected questions about participation and spending with a broader concern: whether the game remained accessible to people with different locations, mobility and caring responsibilities.

This article documents that project, including the subsequent April 21, 2023 report. Its findings describe the people who responded to the survey; they should not be treated as a representative estimate of all Pokémon GO players.

## Importance of the Survey

A game can measure raid attendance and purchases while missing why people stop taking part. The survey aimed to make those constraints visible by asking about gameplay experience, local communities, accessibility, social interaction and communication.

Travel was another question. If a player substitutes driving for remote participation, there may be an environmental cost. The survey can surface that behavior, but it does not establish total emissions or prove that the policy caused every reported change.

## Survey Scope and Results

The project’s April 21 summary recorded **710 participants from 51 countries** and an Inclusion Score of **64.30 out of 100**. It also reported concern about environmental, social and governance issues and changes in intended purchasing and participation.

The archive contains different reporting snapshots. The [public Google Sheet](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1n_GxYFPeTbPFGWkpaUGptPk72pDG6cvYy33jcqUBn9o/edit?usp=sharing), labelled April 14, 2023, records **578 responses from 46 countries** and an Inclusion Score of **64.93**. These figures should be cited with their snapshot date rather than combined into one dataset.

The main value of the responses is in identifying questions for further investigation: who has a local raid community, who relies on remote access, and how the change affects the experience of taking part. Reported intentions to spend less or quit are different from observed purchases or departures.

## Methodology

The survey was offered in multiple languages. The project aggregated responses and summarized ratings of participation, satisfaction, communication and related topics. Its published Inclusion Index combines an Accessibility Score and a Suitability Score through a simple average.

That index is a project-specific measure. A score on a 100-point scale should not be interpreted as the percentage of players who are included, nor as a validated universal measure of accessibility.

Participation was voluntary. Recruitment channels, translation choices, question wording, missing answers and the timing of responses can influence the results. A careful reuse of the findings should state the sample size for each question and preserve the underlying wording and scoring method.

## Implications and Moving Forward

The survey provides a starting point for player interviews and more targeted product research. It suggests that designers should examine the needs behind participation patterns, rather than relying on a single aggregate activity number.

For a follow-up, I would separate actual behavior from intended behavior, compare people with and without local raid communities, and ask travel questions that distinguish dedicated raid journeys from trips made for other reasons. Publishing those definitions alongside the results would make the findings easier to assess and reuse.

## Closing Thoughts

Community feedback is most useful when its scope is clear. The GO Raiding project preserves a set of player experiences from a specific policy change. Its role is to inform discussion and better questions, without asking a voluntary survey to establish more than its design supports.
