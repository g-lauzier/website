Choosing a license for AI-assisted art starts with identifying what can be licensed. A file may combine human-authored composition, generated imagery, licensed source material and edits made by several people. Those elements do not necessarily have the same rights holder or legal status.

The answer also depends on jurisdiction. In the United States, the [Copyright Office’s 2025 report on copyrightability](https://copyright.gov/newsnet/2025/1060.html) says protection requires sufficient human authorship; prompts alone do not establish authorship of the generated expression. Human selection, arrangement or modification can matter. This is a later clarification included in this revision of the original 2023 article, rather than a statement of one worldwide rule.

## Standard licenses and the rights behind them

A public license can make permitted reuse clear when the licensor controls the relevant rights. For example, [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/) allows sharing and adaptation, including commercial use, subject to attribution and its other terms. It is a broad permission, not a mechanism for negotiating a different fee with every reuser.

Creative Commons explains that its licenses operate through copyright and related rights. Applying one [does not create rights in material that lacks them](https://creativecommons.org/faq/). Nor does it clear rights belonging to someone else. The terms of an AI service, a source-image license, privacy or personality rights, and the intended use may need separate consideration.

Keep a record of the process: source files, permissions, model or service used, collaborators and significant human edits. That record helps explain the scope of the proposed license without claiming certainty the process cannot support.

## Custom Licensing Agreements

A custom agreement can address a specific use, such as a print edition, a fashion collection or an architectural installation. Define the material being delivered and the permitted media, territory, term, adaptation rights and exclusivity. State how attribution, payment and approval of changes will work.

For an AI-assisted commission, separate promises about the delivered work from promises about the underlying model or training data. A creator should not warrant ownership of components they do not control. Likewise, an exclusivity clause needs to identify the rights and material to which it applies.

These are issues to resolve in drafting, rather than a ready-made contract suitable for every project. A consequential commercial agreement warrants jurisdiction-specific legal review.

## Collective Licensing Models

A collaboration needs an agreement among its contributors before it can offer a coherent license to customers. Identify who may approve a deal, who can grant which rights, how revenue and costs are shared, and what happens if a contributor leaves.

A shared registry or payment system can record those decisions and help administer them. It cannot establish that the contributors owned the rights they promised. Automated royalty distribution is only as reliable as the agreement, inputs and payment mechanisms behind it.

## Conclusion

A useful license gives the recipient a clear permission and gives the creator a commitment they can stand behind. For AI-assisted work, clarity comes from identifying the contributions and limits first, then choosing the terms that fit the actual use.
