For a smaller bank, cryptocurrency services can be a way to serve a specific client need. They are also an operating commitment: custody, execution, customer support, compliance, and incident response must work together. The decision should begin with a defined service and a credible business case.

“Crypto as a service” can mean very different things. Referring a client to an external provider is different from executing trades, holding private keys, or offering staking. Each model allocates responsibility differently, so the first step is to decide what the bank intends to provide.

## Meeting Client Demand

Start with evidence from the bank's own clients. Are they asking for secure custody, conversion between assets, reporting, or a speculative trading interface? How often would they use the service, and what problems do their existing arrangements create?

A narrow, well-supported use case is easier to evaluate than a broad assumption that younger customers all want cryptocurrency. Interviews and a limited pilot can test willingness to pay, support requirements, and the reasons clients would prefer a bank to another provider.

## Technological Advancements

The technical work extends beyond connecting an API. The bank needs asset and network selection, transaction monitoring, reconciliation, key management, and procedures for network interruptions or unusual token behavior.

Outsourcing can reduce the amount of infrastructure built internally, but it does not eliminate the need to understand the service. The bank should be able to trace an asset from a client's instruction to execution, custody, and withdrawal, including the points where a third party takes control.

## Market Differentiation

The strongest differentiation may be continuity with the relationship clients already have: understandable reporting, clear accountability, and support when something goes wrong. That is more defensible than promising innovation simply because a product involves a blockchain.

Define the service promise narrowly. If the bank offers custody, explain the custody arrangement. If execution depends on an external venue, explain how orders are handled and what happens when that venue is unavailable. Clients should not have to infer these distinctions from marketing language.

## Regulatory Preparedness

Authorization and applicable obligations need to be assessed before launch. The analysis depends on jurisdiction, client type, asset, and activity. There is no universal rule that offering a crypto service is permitted because another bank offers something with a similar name.

For a current Swiss reference, [FINMA Guidance 01/2026 on custody of cryptobased assets](https://www.finma.ch/en/~/media/finma/dokumente/dokumentencenter/myfinma/4dokumentation/finma-aufsichtsmitteilungen/20260112-finma-aufsichtsmitteilung-01-2026.pdf?sc_lang=en) addresses custody arrangements and supervisory treatment. Staking introduces additional considerations; [FINMA's 2023 guidance](https://www.finma.ch/en/news/2023/12/20231220-meldung-am-staking/) highlights risks including slashing, lock-up periods, and the legal position in a provider's bankruptcy. These references update the regulatory context of this article's original 2023 publication.

## Risk Diversification

Adding an asset class does not automatically reduce a client's risk. The outcome depends on position size, correlations under stress, liquidity, and the client's liabilities and objectives. A service provider should not substitute a diversification slogan for a suitability assessment.

The bank also acquires its own risks: operational failures, counterparty exposure, reputational harm, and dependencies on external infrastructure. The [Basel framework's cryptoasset chapter](https://www.bis.org/committees/bcbs/basel-framework/standard/sco/60/inforce/2026-01-01/published/2024-11-27) distinguishes prudential treatment and risk-management expectations for different exposures. Its application depends on the institution and relevant local implementation.

## Enhancing Revenue Streams

Model revenue at the service level. Custody fees, transaction charges, and any retained staking rewards have different cost drivers. Include security operations, insurance where applicable, vendor charges, compliance work, client support, and the cost of handling incidents.

Staking rewards are not equivalent to deposit interest. The underlying asset can lose value, rewards can vary, and access may be delayed by unstaking rules. Clear disclosure is part of the product, not an appendix to the sales pitch.

A useful pilot establishes whether the bank can deliver the intended experience at an acceptable cost before committing to a broader offering.

## Operational Efficiency

Automation can improve reconciliation and reduce repeated manual work when systems share a reliable record. It can also make errors move faster. Strong controls still need to cover transaction approvals, privileged access, address management, accounting, and exception handling.

Test routine withdrawal as carefully as onboarding. Then test an unavailable provider, a disputed instruction, a compromised credential, and a network interruption. These exercises reveal whether responsibility has been allocated clearly between the bank and its suppliers.

## Conclusion

Crypto services can be a useful extension of a bank's offering when a real client need matches the institution's capabilities and risk appetite. They are not a business imperative for every bank.

The decision becomes clearer when it is specific: which service, for which clients, under which custody and regulatory arrangement, at what cost, and with what recovery procedure. Answering those questions is how a bank turns interest in a new asset class into a service it can stand behind.
