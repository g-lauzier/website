For [GeneratedArt](https://generatedart.com), I wanted to understand the system behind a work as well as the result. The selection framework I set out in 2023 focused on artists who developed generative processes or made a substantial creative contribution through data and collaboration.

That focus is a curatorial choice. It does not make other forms of digital art less valuable. It identifies the kind of practice the project was designed to examine: work whose rules, inputs and variations can be explained, discussed and adapted with the artist.

A convincing application does not need the most complex algorithm. It needs a clear account of what the artist contributes and why the chosen process matters to the work.

## Selection Criteria for GeneratedArt Candidates

### 1. Algorithm Development

Explain the rules that generate the work. What enters the system, what changes during execution and what determines the output? Identify the decisions that belong to you and distinguish them from functions supplied by an existing library, model or platform.

A small example is often more useful than a long technical inventory. Show how changing one parameter affects the result, or how the same rules produce several related outputs. If a seed or configuration reproduces a selected work, include it in the documentation.

The originality may lie in the interaction of familiar techniques. There is no need to claim that every underlying mathematical method is new. Accurate attribution makes the distinctive contribution easier to see.

### 2. Inspiration

Describe the question or experience that led to the project. An influence can be another artwork, a physical process, a dataset, a place or an interest in a particular formal problem.

Connect that inspiration to a decision in the work. If you are exploring erosion, explain how that idea affects the rules or materials. If the project concerns surveillance, explain how its treatment of data supports the argument. Naming an influence is a beginning; showing how you worked with it makes the account useful.

Include references where they help the reader understand that relationship. Inspiration, quotation and reuse should be credited at the level each requires.

### 3. Technical Proficiency

List the tools and versions that matter to running or presenting the work, then explain their role. A language or framework name does not tell a curator whether the project is an interactive installation, a live performance, a fixed recording or a printable system.

Describe practical requirements: resolution, rendering time, hardware, external services and any dependencies that might disappear. Explain how the work behaves if an input is unavailable. For an installation, identify who can operate and maintain it.

Technical proficiency is visible in those choices and in the ability to explain their limits. It does not depend on using a particular language or the newest tool.

### 4. Collaboration

Name collaborators and describe their contributions. Algorithm design, visual direction, data preparation, sound, fabrication and installation are different forms of work. Credits should reflect the actual arrangement.

Also explain who can authorize exhibition or licensing and how contributors will be involved in adaptations. A curatorial conversation can otherwise turn into a rights dispute when a project moves into a new format.

If your principal contribution is the concept, data or presentation and another person develops the code, make that clear. The collaboration is part of the project’s authorship, not something to hide behind a single technical credit.

### 5. Data Provisioning

Identify where the inputs come from and how they are transformed. For external data, record the source, collection period, relevant permissions and known limitations. Explain missing values, filtering and any decision that changes what the output represents.

For personal or sensitive data, describe the intended use and how people’s information is protected. A work that criticizes surveillance should still account for its own collection practices. Aggregating a dataset can reduce exposure, but that alone is not proof that every privacy or licensing issue has been resolved.

Procedural or abstract inputs also deserve documentation. Generated numbers may avoid some issues associated with personal data, but code, fonts, textures or other components can still have licensing conditions. “Abstract” is an aesthetic description, not a blanket exemption from rights questions.

### Contributions beyond code {#note}

These criteria allow for different kinds of contribution. An artist does not have to do every task alone. A clear process, honest credits and meaningful decisions matter more than a claim to complete technical independence.

## Licensing and Beyond: Original Algorithms and Their Impact

The interest in original processes was connected to possible applications in fashion, architecture, decoration and other media. A system that produces variations can be adapted to different dimensions or materials, provided those adaptations preserve the character of the work and meet the practical brief.

Before discussing an application, specify what is being offered: a fixed image, a defined edition, access to a generator, a commissioned variation or an ongoing service. Those arrangements create different expectations for delivery, maintenance, exclusivity and reuse.

Third-party tools can be part of the process. The relevant questions are whether the artist can explain their contribution, document the applicable terms and provide the control required by the intended use. General claims that a tool is inherently authentic, inauthentic or incapable of scaling do not answer those questions.

[Creative Commons’ licensing guidance](https://creativecommons.org/faq/) is a useful reminder that permission must come from someone who controls the relevant rights. A description of an algorithm, or a token linked to a work, cannot substitute for that chain of permission.

## Conclusion

The purpose of this framework was to make a generative practice understandable enough to discuss, exhibit and potentially adapt. A strong submission connects intent, method and result, with enough documentation to let someone else engage with the work responsibly.
