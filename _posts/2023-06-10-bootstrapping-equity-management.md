Founders often ask how to finance the next stage of a company without giving away too much of it. The useful answer starts with a more specific question: what milestone will the money buy, and how will reaching it change the business?

Bootstrapping and external investment are financing choices with different costs. Bootstrapping preserves ownership but can constrain speed and place more financial risk on the founders. Investment can fund a step the business cannot yet finance itself, while changing ownership, rights, and expectations. The task is to choose deliberately.

## Understanding Equity Traps

An equity trap is not simply a low ownership percentage. It is a financing structure that leaves the company or its founders poorly positioned for what comes next: insufficient incentives, incompatible investor rights, an unclear cap table, or too little cash to reach a meaningful milestone.

A larger percentage of a company unable to progress is not automatically a better outcome than a smaller percentage of one that can. Conversely, raising capital on attractive headline terms can be costly if the round funds activity without reducing an important business risk.

Before discussing a valuation, write down the milestone, the spending required, the assumptions behind that spending, and the options if progress takes longer than expected.

## Bootstrapping: A Pathway to Self-Funding

Bootstrapping is most useful when customer revenue or modest initial investment can finance the next learning step. Practical approaches include:

- **Start with a paid problem.** A narrow service or product can establish demand before a larger platform is built.
- **Manage cash timing.** Where customers accept them, deposits, milestone payments, and shorter collection cycles can reduce the financing gap. They also create delivery obligations that need to be funded.
- **Keep fixed commitments proportionate.** Delay spending that depends on demand you have not yet demonstrated.
- **Separate cash from profit.** Revenue booked today may be collected later; a profitable contract can still strain working capital.
- **Set a personal risk limit.** A business plan should show how much founder capital is at risk and what would trigger a change of course.

The goal is not austerity for its own sake. It is to spend on evidence and capability that make the next decision easier.

## Equity Management

A cap table should describe the ownership that exists and the ownership already promised. Include issued shares, options, warrants, and instruments that may convert. Then model plausible future rounds rather than reading the current percentages in isolation.

Consider a simplified priced round with no options, convertible instruments, fees, or other adjustments. If a company raises €1 million at a €4 million pre-money valuation, its post-money valuation is €5 million. The new investor owns 20%, and existing shareholders together retain 80%.

If a later round issues another 20% of the company to new investors, that original group retains 80% × 80% = **64%**. Two rounds of 20% dilution do not subtract forty percentage points from the starting ownership; they compound. An option-pool expansion or conversion of earlier instruments would change this example.

| Simplified stage | Original shareholders | First-round investor | Second-round investor |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Before financing | 100% | — | — |
| After the first round | 80% | 20% | — |
| After the second round | 64% | 16% | 20% |

Use this kind of model to ask what the company receives for each change in ownership. Also examine voting rights, board arrangements, liquidation preferences, and reserved decisions. Economic ownership and control are connected, but a percentage alone does not describe both.

## Bootstrapping and Beyond

Some businesses need substantial capital before they can generate revenue. Others face a time-sensitive opportunity or a development cycle that customer payments cannot support. In those cases, insisting on bootstrapping can become a constraint rather than a discipline.

Compare funding paths against the same operating plan. What can the company accomplish from revenue? What changes with additional capital? Which costs are reversible if demand disappoints? A funding decision is stronger when these questions have answers beyond “we could hire more people.”

## Avoiding the Equity Trap through Equity Management

SAFEs and convertible notes can defer aspects of a priced equity round, but they do not remove dilution. The valuation cap, discount, conversion mechanics, and other outstanding instruments can materially change the eventual ownership.

[Y Combinator's SAFE documents and explanation](https://www.ycombinator.com/safe) describe a right to future equity, not a shield against dilution. SAFEs and notes are also different instruments; a note may include interest and maturity terms. Model the actual documents and the company's jurisdiction with the relevant advisers before relying on a simplified percentage.

Keep an updated record of every financing commitment. For each proposed round, compare at least the intended case and a case in which the next raise happens later or on less favorable terms. Make the treatment of an option pool explicit, since a pre-financing pool increase can change who bears the dilution.

## The Long-term Vision

The aim is a company that can keep building, attract the people it needs, and sustain a workable relationship among its owners. Preserving equity matters because it preserves participation in that future. Financing matters because the company needs resources to reach it.

Treat each round as a decision about both the operating plan and the ownership structure. Raise against a clear purpose, keep the cap table understandable, and judge the result by the capability the capital creates.
